Chapter 2
The Transformer — the story of one sentence

We're going to follow a single sentence through the machine, step by step, and watch what happens to it. By the end, you'll be able to picture the whole thing — no wall of math required.

All you need from Chapter 1

Two things: words become lists of numbers (vectors), and the dot product measures how similar two vectors are. That's it. Everything here builds on just those two ideas.

The setupSlide 02

Meet our sentence

"The cat sat on the mat because it was…"

The model has one job: guess the next word. A good guess is "tired" or "soft". But notice something — to guess well, the model has to know what "it" refers to.

to predict the next word, "it" needs to know its own meaning — which lives in an earlier word

Keep this sentence in mind. The whole chapter is just: how does the machine let "it" find "cat"?

The setupSlide 03

The problem: every word is alone

From Chapter 1, each word is turned into a vector (a list of numbers). But a plain neural network processes each word's vector separately — like people in soundproof booths.

each booth does its own math — "it" has no way to reach "cat"
The transformer's one big invention — attention — is simply a way to let the booths pass notes to each other. Everything else in this chapter is plumbing around that idea.
The conveyor beltSlide 04

Every word carries a backpack

Here's the picture to hold for the rest of the course. Each word rides a conveyor belt through the machine, carrying a backpack (its vector). At each station, the machine peeks inside and adds a new note to the backpack. Nothing is ever thrown away.

the backpack only ever gains notes — by addition

The official name for this backpack-on-a-belt is the residual stream. When you see that phrase anywhere, just think: the word's backpack.

The conveyor beltSlide 05

Why "only adding" is such a big deal

At the end of the belt, the backpack is just a pile of notes: the original word + note from station 1 + note from station 2 + … Nothing was overwritten or shredded.

the final answer is a sum — so you can ask "which note did that?"
Why interpretability researchers love this

Because the final state is a simple sum, you can pull out any single note and ask: "did the model say 'tired' because of this note?" You can even remove one note and re-run the model to check. Half the tools in later chapters are just clever ways of doing exactly that.

The conveyor beltSlide 06

One small fix: number the words

"Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" contain the same words — order is what makes them different. So before the belt starts, each backpack gets a stamp: "I am word #1", "I am word #2", and so on.

now the machine can tell who bit whom

That's the whole idea. (The fancy names for how the stamp is made — "positional encoding," "RoPE" — are details you can safely wave at for now.)

The conveyor beltSlide 07

The belt has only two kinds of station

Here's the surprisingly simple truth: the whole transformer is two kinds of station, repeated over and over.

the entire architecture: talk, think, talk, think…
Sticky-note version: attention moves information between words. The MLP thinks about it. If you remember one sentence from this chapter, make it this one.
The meeting room (attention)Slide 08

Inside the meeting room

"It" walks into the meeting room and asks: "I'm a pronoun. Which of you is the thing I refer to?"

attention = asking a question and seeing who matches

That's genuinely all attention is: each word asks a question, every other word answers "that's me!" or stays quiet, and the asker copies notes from whoever matched best. Now let's see the three moving parts that make it work.

The meeting room (attention)Slide 09

Three cards per word: Q, K, V

In the meeting room, every word makes three little cards from its backpack:

Query = question · Key = name tag · Value = the note that gets shared

Each card is made by multiplying the backpack by a learned matrix (W_Q, W_K, W_V). Training is what fills those matrices in — the model learns what questions are worth asking.

The meeting room (attention)Slide 10

Matching: question meets name tags

How does "it" find its match? It compares its question card against everyone's name tag — using the dot product (Chapter 1's similarity score). Bigger score = better match.

line thickness = match strength (a dot product)

The scores then get squashed into percentages that add up to 100% (that's all "softmax" means): cat 80%, mat 15%, sat 5%. Think of it as "it" deciding how to split its attention.

The meeting room (attention)Slide 11

Copying: the notes get shared

Final step. "It" collects a blend of everyone's notes, weighted by those percentages — so it mostly receives cat's note — and drops the blend into its own backpack.

after the meeting, "it" carries cat's meaning in its backpack
The whole meeting, in one line

Ask a question → compare with name tags → split 100% of attention by match quality → collect the weighted blend of notes → add it to your backpack. That is attention. You now understand it.

The meeting room (attention)Slide 12

The famous formula — you already know it

This is the most cited equation in modern AI. Scary-looking, but you just watched every piece of it happen in the meeting room:

four ingredients, all of which you just saw as a story

The √d bit is just a volume knob — without it the scores get so big that one word hogs 100% of the attention and learning stalls. Engineering detail; don't dwell on it.

The meeting room (attention)Slide 13

Many meetings at once: "heads"

One meeting can only settle one kind of question. So each attention station actually runs several small meetings in parallel — each called a head — and each head learns its own specialty.

each head is a small specialist; their notes all get added in

Researchers have found real heads with nameable jobs — "look at the previous word," "find earlier copies of me." A head is small enough that a human can actually figure out what it does. That's why heads are a favorite unit of study.

The meeting room (attention)Slide 14

One house rule: no peeking ahead

The model is being trained to guess the next word. If words could look forward, they could just read the answer — cheating. So the meeting room has a rule: you may only talk to words before you.

the "causal mask" — a one-way mirror facing backward

This is why chat models generate text left to right, one word at a time. The official term is causal mask, but it's just this wall.

The study desk (MLP)Slide 15

The study desk: thinking it over

After the meeting, each word sits alone at a desk and processes what it collected. This is the MLP station. No talking — just each word working on its own backpack.

MLP = spot a pattern in the backpack, write the matching note
What lives here

The MLPs are where most of the model's facts and knowledge seem to be stored — like a giant library of "if you see this pattern, add this note" cards. About two-thirds of the model's learned numbers live in the MLPs.

The study desk (MLP)Slide 16

A warning for later: messy filing

You'd hope each "card" in the MLP library means one clean thing — one card for French, one for DNA, one for cats. Reality is messier: most cards are shared between several unrelated topics.

one card, three totally unrelated jobs — this is called being "polysemantic"

Why the mess? The model wants to track more ideas than it has cards, so it squeezes several ideas onto each one. Chapter 3 makes this the main character — for now, just remember: you can't read the model one card at a time.

Putting it togetherSlide 17

One full "block" = meeting + desk

A meeting room followed by a study desk is called a block. In code, an entire block is literally two lines:

# x = every word's backpack x = x + meeting_room(x) # attention: words share notes x = x + study_desk(x) # MLP: each word thinks alone

Notice both lines say x + … — always adding to the backpack, never replacing it. (There's also a small "volume normalizer" before each station that keeps the numbers from blowing up. It's called layer norm. That's all you need to know about it.)

The punchline

GPT, Claude, Llama — every one of them is this same two-line block, copy-pasted 30 to 100 times. There is no hidden "reasoning module." Understand the block and you've seen the entire machine.

Putting it togetherSlide 18

Stacking blocks: meaning builds floor by floor

Why repeat the block dozens of times? Because each pass lets the backpacks get one step richer — like a building where each floor does more sophisticated work on what came up from below.

rough but useful: basics low, meaning in the middle, decisions on top

This is why researchers constantly ask "at which layer does the model figure X out?" — they're asking which floor does that work.

Putting it togetherSlide 19

The ending: turning a backpack into a word

After the last block, the model takes the final backpack of the last word ("was") and compares it — dot product again! — against a card for every word in the vocabulary. Best matches win.

scores → percentages → pick a word → append it → run the whole thing again
A lovely bonus

Since "backpack → word scores" is just a comparison, you can sneakily apply it to a backpack from any middle floor and ask "what would the model say so far?" — literally watching the answer form floor by floor. That trick is called the logit lens, and it's your first real tool in Chapter 4.

Take a moment here. If you can walk through this picture in your head — backpacks, meetings, desks, compare, pick — you understand the transformer.

A real discoverySlide 21

A real discovered trick: the copy-cat team

The text says "…Harry Potter…" early on. Later, the model sees "Harry" again. What does it guess next? "Potter" — it copies what happened last time.

Researchers opened up a model and found exactly two heads working as a team to do this:

the "induction" team: a note-taker head + a look-up-and-copy head
Why this story matters

This wasn't a guess — researchers verified the actual mechanism, step by step. It proved that inside the giant number-soup there are real, understandable little machines waiting to be found. That's the founding success story of this whole field.

WrapSlide 22

Three myths to leave behind

myth 1
"The model plans its whole answer." Nope — one word at a time, every time. What looks like planning emerges from very good next-word guessing.
myth 2
"Each neuron means one thing." Mostly false (slide 16's messy filing). The single most important correction to carry forward.
myth 3
"Attention maps show why the model did it." They show where notes moved, not what was in them or whether they mattered. Suggestive, not proof.
Habit to build

Whenever an explanation of a model feels neat and satisfying, ask: "how would I actually check that?" This instinct is the real skill the later chapters train.
