Foundational techniques

The hands-on toolkit. Six techniques you'll actually run: the logit lens, direct logit attribution, linear probes, activation patching, steering vectors, and chain-of-thought faithfulness. Each is grounded in the geometry and architecture you already own — and each comes with the baselines and skepticism that separate a real result from a mirage.

OrientationSlide 02

The "start simple" ethos

Before any technique: the order you reach for tools matters as much as the tools themselves. Neel's most repeated advice is try the dumbest thing first.

The escalation ladder

1. Just prompt the model / read its chain-of-thought. → 2. A simple linear probe or the logit lens. → 3. Activation patching / steering. → 4. Heavy machinery (SAEs, custom circuits). Climb only as high as the question forces you. Most good projects live on rungs 1–3.

This chapter presents techniques roughly in that order of complexity. Notice how much you can learn from the cheap ones before the expensive ones earn their keep.

Chapter 4 goals

  • Run the logit lens in your head: project the residual stream to vocabulary at any layer.
  • Use direct logit attribution to say which component pushed which token.
  • Train and — crucially — correctly interpret a linear probe, including its traps.
  • Design an activation patching experiment: clean run, corrupted run, swap, measure.
  • Build a steering vector and test a direction causally.
  • Explain CoT faithfulness and how to test whether reasoning is real.
Recurring refrain

Every technique gets a "baselines & pitfalls" treatment. A technique you can run but can't sanity-check is a liability, not a skill.

Part 1 · Reading the stream — lensesSlide 04

The logit lens

The simplest window into a model. Recall (Chapter 2) that the unembedding W_U turns the final residual vector into logits. The logit lens just applies W_U to the residual stream at an earlier layer.

The whole method\[ \text{logit lens at layer }\ell = W_U \cdot \text{residual}_\ell \]

Because the residual stream lives in one consistent space (Chapter 2, slide 33), this is legal at any depth. You get "what token would the model predict if it had to answer right now, from layer ℓ."

No training, a few lines of code, and you can watch the model's guess evolve layer by layer.

Part 1 · Reading the stream — lensesSlide 05

What the logit lens shows — and its limits

Typical finding: the correct answer's probability is near zero in early layers, then climbs and locks in over the middle-to-late layers. You can literally see where in depth a fact gets retrieved or a decision made.

Limits — don't overtrust it

It assumes early-layer representations are already in the "final answer" coordinate system, which isn't quite true — early layers may encode things the unembedding can't read yet. So the logit lens can be blurry or misleading in early layers, and sometimes shows nothing where information genuinely exists in a different form.

Part 1 · Reading the stream — lensesSlide 06

The tuned lens: a learned upgrade

The tuned lens fixes the logit lens's early-layer blurriness by learning a small per-layer correction (an affine map) that translates each layer's representation into the final coordinate system before unembedding.

Trade-off

More faithful readouts across depth, at the cost of a little training and the risk that the learned map "invents" structure that wasn't really there (a probing-style caveat — slide 13). Rule of thumb: logit lens for a quick look; tuned lens when you need trustworthy early-layer readings. Both are cheap relative to what they reveal.

These "lenses" are the ancestors of the cutting-edge readout methods (J-Lens, NLAs) in Chapter 6 — all variations on "decode the residual stream into something human-readable."

Part 2 · AttributionSlide 07

Direct logit attribution (DLA)

Question: which component pushed the model toward this particular token? DLA answers it by exploiting the residual stream's additivity (Chapter 2, slide 32).

The idea

The final logit for a token is a dot product of the final residual state with that token's unembedding direction. Since the residual state is a sum of every component's contribution, the logit is also a sum: each component contributes its own (its output) · (token direction). Read off the per-component terms → you know who voted for the token, and how much.

So you can produce a ranked list: "head 9.6 contributed +2.1 to the logit for 'Paris,' MLP 10 contributed +0.8, head 3.2 contributed −0.4." Attribution without any intervention.

Part 2 · AttributionSlide 08

DLA in practice — and its blind spot

DLA is a fast way to localise which late-layer components most directly move a logit. Great first pass for "who's responsible for this output?"

The blind spot

DLA only sees direct effects — a component that writes straight to the logit. A component that matters indirectly (it sets up a feature that a later component uses) contributes ~0 in DLA yet may be essential. DLA under-credits early and indirect contributors.

The fix

For indirect effects you need causal methods that trace through the network — activation patching and path patching (Part 4). DLA tells you the direct voters; patching tells you the whole causal chain. Use DLA to generate hypotheses, patching to confirm them.

Part 3 · ProbingSlide 09

Linear probes

A linear probe tests whether a concept is linearly represented at some layer. You take activations from many inputs, label them (concept present / absent), and train a simple linear classifier — literally, learn a direction w such that w · activation is high when the concept is present.

A probe is one dot product\[ \text{score} = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{h} + b \quad\rightarrow\quad \text{concept present if score}>0 \]

This is exactly the "read off a concept with a dot product" idea from Chapter 1, slide 12 — now with w found by fitting labelled data instead of being handed to you.

Part 3 · ProbingSlide 10

Training a probe (the recipe)

  1. Pick a layer and a concept ("text is French," "user is angry," "answer will be refused").
  2. Gather inputs with known labels; run the model; save the activation at that layer for each.
  3. Fit a linear classifier (logistic regression) from activation → label.
  4. Evaluate on held-out data — accuracy tells you how linearly decodable the concept is there.
What a good result means

High held-out accuracy ⇒ the concept is (linearly) present in the representation at that layer. That's genuinely useful — it's evidence for the linear representation hypothesis in this specific case, and gives you a direction to steer or monitor with.

Part 3 · ProbingSlide 11

The probing trap you must internalise

Present ≠ used

A probe finding a concept proves the information is there. It does not prove the model uses it for anything. Powerful probes can extract information the model itself ignores.

Worse, a sufficiently expressive probe can pick up on spurious correlations in your dataset rather than a real feature. "My probe decodes deception with 95% accuracy" might mean "my deceptive examples happened to be longer."

Why Neel cares

Confusing "decodable" with "causally used" is one of the most common overclaims in interp. Demonstrating use requires an intervention (Part 4). Probing generates the hypothesis; causal methods earn the claim.

Part 3 · ProbingSlide 12

Baselines for probes

Because probes over-claim so easily, they demand baselines. Standard ones:

  • Random direction: does a random w get near your accuracy? If so, your "feature" may be an artefact of the data's structure.
  • Control task: can the probe also fit random labels? If a probe can memorise noise, high accuracy on your real task means less.
  • Simple features: can bag-of-words or input length predict the label without the model at all? If so, you haven't shown the model represents it.
  • Layer sweep: where does accuracy peak? A sensible depth-profile is reassuring; flat-everywhere is suspicious.
Keep it in scope

Probes are still fantastic — cheap, fast, first rung of the ladder. Just report them with baselines and phrase conclusions as "linearly decodable," not "the model thinks."

Part 3 · ProbingSlide 13

Probes as a monitoring tool

Beyond analysis, probes are a leading candidate for practical safety monitoring: train a probe for "harmful intent," "the model is about to hallucinate," or "eval awareness," then watch it fire at runtime — a cheap internal alarm.

This is squarely in Neel's "applied interp"

A probe-based monitor that beats a prompted-classifier baseline on a real detection task is exactly the kind of pragmatic, useful, baselined result he's excited by. If you're hunting for a project shape, "can a simple probe reliably detect X, better than just asking the model?" is a strong template.

Part 4 · Causal interventionsSlide 14

The core upgrade: intervene, don't just watch

Everything so far has been mostly observational. Now the gold standard (Chapter 3, slide 25): change something inside the model and measure the effect on the output. This is what lets you claim causation, not correlation.

The unfair advantage of interp

Unlike a neuroscientist, you can freely read, write, freeze, and swap any activation, and re-run as many times as you like with perfect control. Causal experiments that are impossible on a brain are a few lines of code on a model. This is the field's core methodological superpower — use it.

Part 4 · Causal interventionsSlide 15

Activation patching: the setup

The workhorse causal method. You need two inputs that differ in the thing you care about:

Clean run

"The Eiffel Tower is in ___" → model says "Paris." Save all internal activations.

Corrupted run

"The Colosseum is in ___" → model says "Rome." A minimal edit that flips the answer.

Now the experiment: take a single activation (one component, one position) from the clean run and paste it into the corrupted run, leaving everything else corrupted. Then see how much the output moves back toward "Paris."

Part 4 · Causal interventionsSlide 16

Activation patching: the mechanics

For each component/position you suspect, run the corrupted input but overwrite that one activation with the value it had in the clean run. Measure the recovery of the clean answer.

  • Big recovery → that activation carries the information that determines the answer. You've localised a causally-important spot.
  • No effect → that activation isn't where the relevant info lives.

Sweep across all layers and positions and you get a heatmap of "where does the answer-deciding information live?"

Part 4 · Causal interventionsSlide 17

Denoising vs noising

Denoising (clean → corrupted)

Patch a clean activation into a corrupted run. Asks: "is this activation sufficient to restore the behaviour?" Finds components that carry the key info.

Noising (corrupted → clean)

Patch a corrupted activation into a clean run. Asks: "is this activation necessary — does breaking it destroy the behaviour?"

Why run both

Sufficiency and necessity are different claims. A rigorous localisation shows a component is both. Reporting only one direction is a subtle way to overclaim — exactly the kind of gap a careful reader (or Neel) will probe.

Part 4 · Causal interventionsSlide 18

Path patching & ablation

Path patching

A refinement that patches the effect of one component only along its path to a specific downstream component, rather than everywhere at once. Lets you test edges of a circuit: "does head 3.2 matter because head 9.6 reads it?"

Ablation

Delete a component's contribution — set it to zero (zero-ablation) or to its average value (mean-ablation) — and see what breaks. A blunt necessity test.

Ablation gotcha

Zero-ablation can push activations "off-distribution" into states the model never normally sees, producing weird effects that don't reflect normal computation. Mean-ablation is often safer. Another reminder that a technique "having an effect" isn't the same as your interpretation being right (Ch3, slide 31).

Part 4 · Causal interventionsSlide 19

Choosing good clean/corrupted pairs

The quality of a patching experiment lives or dies on the input pair. The edit between clean and corrupted should isolate exactly the variable you care about and nothing else.

  • Minimal: change as little as possible (ideally one token) so the localisation is clean.
  • Matched length/structure: so position indices line up and you're not confounded by shape differences.
  • Reliable behaviour: the model should robustly give different answers on the two inputs, or there's nothing to recover.
This is where taste shows

Designing the right counterfactual is a craft skill — and a place where a thoughtful human clearly adds value over "ask the model." Sloppy pairs give confident-looking but meaningless heatmaps.

Part 5 · SteeringSlide 20

Steering vectors

If a concept is a direction (Chapter 1, slide 9; Chapter 3), you should be able to add that direction into the residual stream and push the model's behaviour toward the concept. That's a steering vector — and it's simultaneously a causal test and a control knob.

The intervention\[ \text{residual}_\ell \;\leftarrow\; \text{residual}_\ell + \alpha\,\mathbf{d} \]

Add α times the concept direction d at layer ℓ during the forward pass. Positive α → more of the concept; negative → less. Pure "add & scale" from Chapter 1, slide 9.

Part 5 · SteeringSlide 21

Where the direction comes from

The most common and robust recipe is difference of means (a.k.a. contrastive / "diff-in-means"):

  1. Collect activations for many inputs with the concept (e.g. angry text) and without it (calm text).
  2. Average each group; subtract: d = mean(with) − mean(without).
  3. That difference vector is your steering direction.
Why this beats fancier methods often

Diff-in-means is dead simple, needs no training, and empirically steers well — a perfect example of "try the dumb thing first." It's frequently a strong baseline that fancier techniques (even SAE-derived directions) struggle to beat. Neel would nod approvingly.

Part 5 · SteeringSlide 22

What steering proves (and doesn't)

If adding d reliably produces the concept and subtracting it removes the concept, you have causal, bidirectional evidence that d is a direction the model actually uses for that concept. Much stronger than a probe alone.

The catch: side effects

Steering often degrades fluency or drags in correlated concepts (steer "happy" and you might also get "exclamation marks!!"). A steering vector is rarely a clean scalpel. Always check the model still produces coherent, on-task output.

Part 5 · SteeringSlide 23

Baselines for steering

  • Random direction of the same norm: does adding noise cause a similar-sized effect? If yes, your effect is just "perturbation," not "this concept."
  • Prompting baseline: can you get the same behaviour change by just asking the model in the prompt? If prompting works as well, steering hasn't earned its complexity.
  • Coherence check: measure whether the steered output is still fluent/on-topic, not just concept-laden gibberish.
  • Dose-response: does the effect scale sensibly with α? A clean monotonic curve is reassuring.
The prompting baseline especially

"Could I have gotten this by prompting?" is Neel's recurring gut-check — it's the "value-add over prompting Claude myself" test. If a steering result can't beat a good prompt, say so honestly. That honesty is itself scored well.

Part 5 · SteeringSlide 24

Steering as safety lever

Beyond analysis, steering is a candidate intervention: dial down a "deception" or "sycophancy" direction at inference, or dial up "refuse harmful requests." Related ideas power activation-level guardrails and "concept editing."

Reality check

It's promising but brittle — side effects, dose sensitivity, and features that don't isolate cleanly all limit reliability today. Presented so you know the safety ambition; treat robust steering-based control as an open problem, not a solved product. Honest framing of maturity is, again, exactly the register that scores well.

Part 6 · Chain-of-thoughtSlide 25

Chain-of-thought, and why it's a safety goldmine

Chain-of-thought (CoT) is when a model writes out intermediate reasoning before its answer ("Let's think step by step…"). It improves performance on hard tasks — and, temptingly, seems to show us the model's reasoning in plain English.

Why safety folk are excited

If the CoT faithfully reflects the model's actual computation, then reading it is free interpretability — a natural-language window into the model's process, no probes needed. Monitoring the CoT for signs of misbehaviour could be a cheap, scalable safety layer. That "if" is the whole ballgame.

Prime Neel territory

CoT faithfulness and CoT monitoring are among his most-excited applied-interp areas. It's black-box-friendly, safety-relevant, and full of tractable projects — a great place to look for an application idea.

Part 6 · Chain-of-thoughtSlide 26

CoT faithfulness: the core question

The problem

The stated reasoning may not be the real reason for the answer. A model can produce a fluent, plausible chain-of-thought that is a post-hoc rationalisation — the decision was made by some internal process the words don't reflect.

This mirrors humans: we confidently explain choices we actually made for other reasons. If CoT is unfaithful, then trusting it for safety monitoring is dangerous — you'd be watching a cover story.

Part 6 · Chain-of-thoughtSlide 27

Evidence that CoT can be unfaithful

  • Hidden cues: plant a subtle hint that fixes the answer (e.g. "a professor thinks it's (B)"). Models often follow the hint but write a CoT that never mentions it — justifying (B) on other grounds.
  • Filler / unused steps: models sometimes reach the right answer even when their stated steps are wrong or irrelevant.
  • Perturbation insensitivity: editing the reasoning sometimes doesn't change the answer the way it should if the reasoning were load-bearing.
Nuance, not doom

CoT isn't always unfaithful — often it genuinely helps and reflects real computation, especially on hard multi-step problems. The research question is when and how much, and how to measure faithfulness — not a blanket verdict. That measurement problem is where the good projects are.

Part 6 · Chain-of-thoughtSlide 28

How to test faithfulness

  • Causal edits to the CoT: change a step and see if the answer changes as it logically should. If the answer is unmoved, that step wasn't load-bearing.
  • Hint injection: insert an answer-fixing cue and check whether the CoT admits it (faithful) or hides it (unfaithful).
  • Resampling (Chapter 5): generate many CoTs for the same prompt and study which steps actually change the outcome — a distributional view of what matters.
  • Cross-check with internals: does a probe/patch agree with what the CoT claims is happening?
The unifying move

Every test is a counterfactual: change the reasoning (or a cue), predict what a faithful model would do, and check. Same interventional spirit as patching, applied to text. Techniques rhyme.

Part 6 · Chain-of-thoughtSlide 29

CoT monitoring and its fragility

A leading safety proposal: monitor the chain-of-thought for signs of scheming, misuse, or dangerous plans. Cheap and scalable if reasoning models externalise their thinking.

Two failure modes to respect

(1) Unfaithfulness — the bad reasoning simply isn't verbalised (this slide's Part). (2) Obfuscation — if you train against the monitor, the model may learn to hide its reasoning or encode it steganographically, defeating the monitor while looking clean. Monitoring pressure can destroy the very signal it reads.

This tension — CoT monitoring is promising and fragile — is one of the liveliest debates in applied safety, and a rich seam for a thoughtful project.

Part 7 · Putting it togetherSlide 30

A mini-investigation with the whole toolkit

Suppose a model sometimes gives a sycophantic answer (agrees with the user even when wrong). A well-shaped investigation:

  1. Prompt/CoT (rung 1): read its reasoning on sycophantic vs honest cases. Form a hypothesis.
  2. Logit lens / DLA (rung 2): where does the sycophantic token get its push? Which layers?
  3. Probe (rung 2): train "user stated a belief" and "about to agree" probes. Where do they fire?
  4. Patch (rung 3): clean = neutral question, corrupted = leading question; localise what flips the answer.
  5. Steer (rung 3): build a sycophancy direction (diff-in-means); test add/subtract, with random + prompting baselines.
  6. Skeptic + distill: dumbest alternative? necessity and sufficiency? write it up, lead with the finding.
Part 7 · Putting it togetherSlide 31

Technique selection guide

"what's it predicting, and where?"
→ logit lens / tuned lens
"which component moved this output?"
→ direct logit attribution (direct effects only)
"is concept X represented here?"
→ linear probe (decodable ≠ used!)
"which activation causes the behaviour?"
→ activation patching (denoise + noise)
"is this a circuit edge?"
→ path patching
"is X necessary?"
→ ablation (prefer mean over zero)
"does the model use direction d? can I control it?"
→ steering vector (diff-in-means)
"is the stated reasoning the real reason?"
→ CoT faithfulness tests
Meta-rule

Observational tools (lens, DLA, probe) generate hypotheses cheaply; interventional tools (patch, ablate, steer) earn the causal claim. Pair them.

Part 7 · Putting it togetherSlide 32

The baselines & skepticism cheat-sheet

Fold these questions into every result before you believe it:

  • Does a random direction / random component do as well? (the noise baseline)
  • Could I get this by just prompting? (the value-add baseline)
  • Did I test both necessity and sufficiency?
  • Is my dataset creating a spurious correlation the method latched onto?
  • What is the dumbest alternative explanation, and did I rule it out?
  • Am I making an existence-proof claim or a general one? (Ch3, slide 30)
This list is the job

Running techniques is the easy part; a capable model can write the code. The scarce skill — and what Neel is selecting for — is the judgment on this slide.

Part 7 · Putting it togetherSlide 33

A note on tooling

In practice these techniques are run with open-source libraries that hook into a model's forward pass to read and edit activations — nnsight, TransformerLens, and similar. You register "hooks" at chosen layers to capture or overwrite the residual stream.

Practical steer for the application

Use capable, modern open models (e.g. Qwen, Gemma, DeepSeek families) — not tiny/old ones like GPT-2, which Neel explicitly discourages because they're too weak to show interesting behaviour. Get a persistent Jupyter/kernel workflow going so you can iterate fast (more on this in Chapter 7). Don't sink your limited hours into infrastructure yak-shaving.
