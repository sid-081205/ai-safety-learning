OrientationSlide 02

From "how it's built" to "what it's doing"

Chapters 1–2 were architecture: the parts and how they connect. Knowing that doesn't tell you what a trained model computes, any more than knowing neurons fire tells you what a brain is thinking.

Mechanistic interpretability is the attempt to bridge that gap — to find the human-understandable algorithms and representations that training wrote into the weights, and to do it rigorously enough to trust the answer.

The definition to hold

Mechanistic interpretability: reverse-engineering the internal computations of neural networks into human-understandable components — features and circuits — with enough evidence (ideally causal) to be confident you're right.

Part 1 · The mechanistic viewSlide 04

"Mechanistic" vs "behavioural"

Behavioural interpretability

Study the model from outside: feed inputs, watch outputs, correlate. "It refuses more when the prompt mentions weapons." Useful, cheap, but it can't distinguish mechanisms that produce the same behaviour.

Mechanistic interpretability

Open the box: identify the internal representations and computations that cause the behaviour. "This direction in layer 12 represents 'harmful request,' and ablating it stops the refusal."

Neel's pragmatic nuance

He's not a purist who insists everything be fully mechanistic. Some of his favourite work is largely behavioural (reading the chain-of-thought, clever prompting) — and he explicitly praises starting with the simplest thing that works. "Mechanistic" is a spectrum, and the goal is understanding that's useful and true, by whatever mix of methods gets you there.

Part 1 · The mechanistic viewSlide 05

Features: the unit of analysis

A feature is a meaningful property the model represents and uses — "the text is in French," "this is a phone number," "the user is angry," "this request is harmful." Features are the nouns of interpretability.

Working definition

A feature is (roughly) a direction in activation space that corresponds to a human-interpretable concept. The model stores it, reads it (dot product), and acts on it (writing related directions downstream). Chapter 1's geometry is the whole reason "feature = direction" is even sayable.

The central bet of the field is that models are built out of features and the circuits connecting them — that beneath the billions of opaque numbers lies a describable vocabulary of concepts. Whether that bet fully pays off is unsettled, but it pays off often enough to be worth pursuing.

Part 1 · The mechanistic viewSlide 06

The Linear Representation Hypothesis

Stated properly

Features are represented as linear directions in activation space. The degree to which a feature is "present" is (approximately) how far the activation extends along that feature's direction — recoverable by a dot product — and features combine by addition.

This is the load-bearing assumption behind most of the toolkit:

  • Probes find a feature's direction and read it off (dot product).
  • Steering adds a scaled feature direction to change behaviour.
  • SAEs assume activations are sparse sums of feature directions.

If the hypothesis were false, all of these would be misguided. It's not always true — but its frequent approximate truth is why the field works at all.

Part 1 · The mechanistic viewSlide 07

Evidence for and against

For

  • king − man + woman ≈ queen and countless similar linear analogies.
  • Linear probes routinely recover concepts with high accuracy.
  • Steering vectors work: add a direction, get the expected behaviour shift.
  • SAEs extract thousands of interpretable directions.

Against / caveats

  • Some features are genuinely nonlinear or multi-dimensional (e.g. certain circular representations like days of the week).
  • A probe finding a direction doesn't prove the model uses it.
  • Directions interfere under superposition (next part).
The right attitude

Treat linearity as a powerful default worth trying first, not dogma. A great instinct — very Neel — is to test the linear hypothesis, and when it breaks, notice and investigate why. That noticing is often where the interesting result hides.

Part 1 · The mechanistic viewSlide 08

Privileged vs non-privileged bases

A subtle but important point. The residual stream has no special coordinate system — you can rotate it freely and the model is unchanged. So there's no reason a single dimension (a "residual stream neuron") should mean anything. Features live along arbitrary directions, not along the axes.

MLP neurons are different: the nonlinearity acts on each neuron separately, which gives the neuron axes a privileged status — so individual MLP neurons are more likely (though far from guaranteed) to be meaningful.

Why you care

This explains why you often can't just "look at neuron 3081." Meaning is in directions you have to find, not read off axis by axis. Probes and SAEs are, in part, tools for discovering the right (non-axis-aligned) directions.

Part 2 · SuperpositionSlide 09

The puzzle: more features than dimensions

A model wants to represent a staggering number of concepts — far more than its few thousand dimensions. If each feature needed its own perpendicular direction, you could only fit d features in d dimensions. Models clearly track many more than that. So how?

The trick: near-orthogonality

In high dimensions there's room for far more almost-perpendicular directions than exactly-perpendicular ones. You can pack thousands of directions into 512 dimensions such that any two have a small dot product. Features can share the space if they mostly don't step on each other.

This packing is called superposition, and it's the defining difficulty of the whole field.

Part 2 · SuperpositionSlide 10

The superposition hypothesis

The claim

Neural networks represent more features than they have dimensions by storing them in overlapping, near-orthogonal directions — effectively simulating a much larger, sparse network inside a smaller dense one.

The cost of packing: features interfere. When one feature is active, it leaks a little noise onto the others (their small-but-nonzero dot products). The model tolerates this because the interference is usually small and the payoff — representing vastly more concepts — is huge.

Consequence

Superposition is why neurons are polysemantic: a single neuron sits at the crossing point of several feature directions, so it lights up for several unrelated concepts. The mess isn't sloppiness — it's an efficient encoding we have to decode.

Part 2 · SuperpositionSlide 11

Picturing it: too many arrows, too little room

In 2-D you can fit only 2 perpendicular directions. But if you allow small overlaps, you can cram in 5, 6, or more directions that are roughly distinct — enough that a dot product still mostly tells them apart.

Scale that intuition to hundreds or thousands of dimensions and the packing capacity explodes. The model exploits this ruthlessly.

Why it barely works

When only a few features are active at once (sparsity — next slide), the interference from all the inactive ones is near zero. Superposition is a bet on sparsity.

near-orthogonal, not orthogonal
Part 2 · SuperpositionSlide 12

Sparsity is what makes it work

The reason superposition is viable: at any moment, only a tiny fraction of all possible features are active. A given token isn't simultaneously about French and DNA and tax law and medieval history. Because active features are sparse, their overlapping directions rarely collide at once.

This is the key that unlocks SAEs

Sparse autoencoders (Chapter 5) are built directly on this: they assume any activation is a sparse sum of a few feature directions drawn from a large dictionary, and they try to recover that dictionary. "Sparse" isn't decoration in the name — it's the whole mechanism, inherited from superposition.

Part 2 · SuperpositionSlide 13

Polysemanticity, precisely

A polysemantic neuron activates for multiple unrelated inputs. It's the surface symptom of superposition: because feature directions don't align with neuron axes, any single neuron is a blend of several features.

The dream of "one neuron = one concept" (monosemanticity) is mostly false in real models — and chasing it neuron-by-neuron is a dead end.

The practical lesson

Do not build an interpretation on "neuron N means X" without heavy checking — it probably also means Y and Z. This is a classic trap. The fix is to find the right direction (probe, SAE feature), not to trust a raw neuron.

Part 2 · SuperpositionSlide 14

Why superposition makes interpretability hard

  • You can't read the model axis by axis — meaning is in tangled directions.
  • Any feature you extract might be contaminated by interference from others.
  • The number of features is huge and unknown; there's no clean list to enumerate.
  • Tools that assume clean separation (naïve neuron analysis) give misleading answers.
Neel's evolving view

Untangling superposition perfectly ("enumerative safety" — list every feature) was an early dream. Neel is now fairly pessimistic about that ambitious version and cooler on "science of SAEs for its own sake." His pivot is toward pragmatic uses that tolerate messiness — you'll feel this stance throughout Chapters 5–7.

Part 3 · CircuitsSlide 15

Circuits: features connected by weights

If features are the nouns, circuits are the sentences. A circuit is a set of features/components wired together by the model's weights to implement a computation — feature A in an early layer causes feature B downstream, which drives an output.

Induction heads, recalled

The induction circuit from Chapter 2 is the canonical example: previous-token head → induction head → copy behaviour. That's a circuit — a small, causally-verified algorithm assembled from the architectural parts. It proved circuits are real and findable.

The grand mechanistic vision was to decompose an entire model into a library of features and the circuits connecting them. Reality is messier — but the concept remains the right way to think about "how a behaviour is implemented."

Part 3 · CircuitsSlide 16

The residual stream as a communication bus

Recall from Chapter 2: components communicate only through the additive residual stream. Circuits are therefore chains of "write to the stream, later read from the stream."

a circuit = writes and reads across the stream

So "find the circuit" becomes concrete: which component writes the relevant feature, and which downstream component reads and uses it? Every causal technique in Chapter 4 is a way to answer that.

Part 3 · CircuitsSlide 17

Composition: how depth builds capability

Components compose in layers of abstraction: early features (token identity, position) feed mid-level features (entities, syntax roles) feed high-level features (sentiment, intent, the answer). Each layer's writes become the next layer's reads.

Analogy with a caveat

It's tempting to compare this to vision networks (edges → textures → objects). The analogy is real but looser in language models — features are more entangled and less cleanly hierarchical. Use it for intuition, not as a precise claim.

Part 3 · CircuitsSlide 18

Motifs and universality

A striking early finding: some circuits and features recur across different models trained independently — induction heads show up everywhere, similar low-level features appear in many vision nets. This is the universality hypothesis: models converge on similar solutions.

If true and widespread, it's great news: understand a circuit once, reuse the understanding. If weak, every model must be studied afresh.

Where it stands

Universality holds for some low-level structure but is patchy for higher-level features. It's a hypothesis to keep in mind, not a guarantee. A good research instinct: when you find something in one model, check whether it replicates in another — both to test universality and to strengthen your claim.

Part 4 · Why — theories of changeSlide 19

Why do this? The safety case

Interpretability isn't just curiosity. The motivating bet: as AI systems become more capable and autonomous, we need ways to verify what they're actually doing that don't rely on trusting their outputs. Behaviour can be faked; mechanism is harder to fake.

Things we'd love to detect from the inside

Deceptive intent · hidden goals · situational/eval awareness · whether a refusal is principled or a pattern-match · whether the chain-of-thought reflects the real computation · early warning of dangerous capabilities. Each is a place where "reading the mind" beats "reading the output."

Part 4 · Why — theories of changeSlide 20

Neel's pragmatic vision

This is essential for the application, so read carefully. Neel has shifted from ambitious "fully reverse-engineer the model" interpretability toward pragmatic interpretability.

Cooled on

  • Complete reverse-engineering / enumerative safety
  • Circuit-finding for its own sake
  • SAE hill-climbing & basic science of SAEs
  • Toy models, grokking, very theoretical work

Excited about

  • Interp that does something useful, measured vs baselines, on models that matter
  • Model biology, model forensics
  • Science of post-training, alignment training, generalisation
  • Better general-purpose methods (J-Lens, NLAs)
His line

"Being able to read an AGI's mind should be extremely useful." The emphasis is on useful, now, with real baselines — not elegance for its own sake.

Part 4 · Why — theories of changeSlide 21

Ambitious vs pragmatic, side by side

Ambitious interp

Goal: a complete, faithful account of everything the model computes — enumerate all features and circuits, "solve" the model. Beautiful, but Neel now thinks it likely doesn't scale to systems that matter, in time to matter.

Pragmatic interp

Goal: use partial understanding to accomplish a concrete, valuable task better than the alternatives — detect misuse, catch deception, debug training, monitor for a concept. Accept messiness; demand usefulness and rigour.

For your project

Frame your application around a pragmatic question with a testable payoff, and compare against a simple baseline. "I understood this behaviour and could act on it" beats "I partially reverse-engineered a circuit." This single reframing aligns you with his current taste.

Part 4 · Why — theories of changeSlide 22

What interpretability could catch that behaviour can't

The sharpest argument for interp is the deceptive alignment worry: a capable model that has learned to behave well while under observation, but pursues something else when it believes it isn't watched. Pure behavioural testing is, by construction, blind to this — the model passes your tests on purpose.

The hope

Internal signals might reveal the mismatch: a "I'm being evaluated" feature firing, a goal representation that contradicts the stated one, a deception direction lighting up. Even imperfect internal detectors raise the cost of hiding. This is why probing for eval-awareness and deception (Chapters 5–6) is taken seriously.

Honest caveat

We're nowhere near a reliable "is it scheming?" detector, and a sufficiently capable model might learn to obscure its internals too. Interp is a promising tool, not a solved solution. Overclaiming here is a real failure mode.

Part 4 · Why — theories of changeSlide 23

Humility: the limits of interpretability

  • Most features are still not understood; we can only interpret a fraction of any model.
  • An interpretation can be a plausible story that isn't actually what's happening (interp illusions — slide 33).
  • Techniques have false positives; a probe can "find" info the model never uses.
  • Scaling understanding to frontier-size models is genuinely unsolved.
Why humility is a selection criterion

Neel prizes truth-seeking and skepticism above almost everything. "Most research results are false, especially the exciting ones." An application that soberly states its limits beats one that overclaims. The humility on this slide isn't hedging — it's the exact disposition he's selecting for.

Part 5 · The landscape of approachesSlide 24

Black-box vs white-box

Black-box

Only inputs and outputs. Prompting, fine-tuning, behavioural evals, reading the chain-of-thought. Cheap, scalable, model-agnostic. Often shockingly effective.

White-box

Access to internals: activations, weights, gradients. Probes, patching, SAEs, lenses. More powerful for causal claims, but needs open weights and more effort.

Start black, go white when needed

Neel's repeated advice: try the simplest thing first — often prompting or reading the CoT "just works," and you stop there. Reach for white-box machinery when the cheap approach hits a wall. Applications that reach for SAEs when a prompt would do read as showing off.

Part 5 · The landscape of approachesSlide 25

Observational vs interventional — the big one

The most important methodological distinction in the field.

Observational

You watch: "this direction correlates with harmfulness." Correlation. Cheap, but can't establish that the model uses the thing you found.

Interventional

You change something and measure the effect: ablate the direction, does refusal stop? Patch the activation, does the answer flip? This tests causation.

Why it decides quality

Interpretability's core epistemic upgrade is that we can run experiments on the model's internals — unlike neuroscience, we have perfect access and control. Causal evidence is the gold standard, and "did you test this causally?" is the question that separates a compelling result from a suggestive one. Chapter 4's patching and steering are the workhorses here.

Part 5 · The landscape of approachesSlide 27

Model biology: Neel's framing

A useful umbrella you'll hear from him: model biology — studying the high-level, qualitative properties of a model's behaviour as if it were an organism you're trying to understand, especially for weird, emergent, or safety-relevant phenomena.

Why "biology"

Biologists don't fully reverse-engineer a cell before learning useful things about it — they observe, perturb, form hypotheses, and build reliable partial models. Model biology imports that mindset: you don't need a complete mechanistic account to make real, testable claims about how a model behaves and why. This framing makes room for pragmatic, mixed-method work — and it's the parent category of model forensics (Chapter 6).

Part 5 · The landscape of approachesSlide 28

Levels of analysis

A single phenomenon can be studied at several levels. Being clear about which level you're claiming something at is a mark of good work:

behaviour
what the model does (outputs, refusals, errors). The starting point.
representation
what it encodes internally (features, directions). "It represents X here."
circuit
how components compute the behaviour. "This head writes X, that MLP reads it."

Most strong applications live at the behaviour→representation boundary: "here's a behaviour, here's the internal thing driving it, here's causal evidence linking them." Full circuit-level accounts are slower and, per Neel, no longer the priority.

Part 6 · Practical groundingSlide 29

What good interp evidence looks like

  • Causal, not just correlational — you intervened and the behaviour changed as predicted.
  • Baselined — compared against a random direction, a control task, or "just ask the model."
  • Specific — a named model, a named component/direction, a concrete number, not vibes.
  • Falsifiable and tested — you stated what would disprove you and checked it.
  • Replicated where feasible — holds in another prompt set or another model.
Straight from the rubric

These map almost one-to-one onto Neel's evaluation criteria: clarity, good taste, truth-seeking/skepticism, technical depth, and simplicity. Internalising this slide is internalising how he grades.

Part 6 · Practical groundingSlide 30

Existence proofs vs general claims

Know which kind of claim you're making — it changes what evidence you need.

Existence proof

"Here's an example of phenomenon X." Cherry-picking is fine — one clear case suffices. Good for "look, this can happen."

General claim

"Method M is the right tool for task T" or "models generally do X." Needs representative samples and baselines. Cherry-picking here is a serious error.

Neel calls this out explicitly

He warns against "relying only on a few cherry-picked qualitative examples" as a major red flag — unless you're honestly making an existence-proof claim. Being crisp about which mode you're in is itself a signal of research maturity.

Part 6 · Practical groundingSlide 31

Interpretability illusions

A sobering genre of result: techniques that look like they reveal a mechanism but are artefacts. The classic is the subspace activation-patching illusion — patching a direction changed the output, seeming to prove that direction was the mechanism, when in fact it worked through an unrelated pathway.

The lesson that generalises

A tool "working" (your intervention had an effect) does not guarantee your interpretation of why is correct. Always ask: what's the dumbest alternative explanation for this effect, and have I ruled it out? This exact instinct — "what's the dumbest way this could be wrong?" — is what Neel means by sanity-checking, and it recurs as the spine of Chapter 7.

Part 6 · Practical groundingSlide 32

A worked mental model of a mini-investigation

Tie the concepts together into how a real inquiry flows:

  1. Behaviour: "The model does something odd — it refuses this benign request."
  2. Cheap first pass: read the chain-of-thought; vary the prompt; form a hypothesis ("it pattern-matches a jailbreak").
  3. Representation: train a probe or find a direction for "perceived jailbreak"; check it fires.
  4. Causal test: ablate/steer that direction — does the refusal change? Compare to a random-direction baseline.
  5. Skeptic pass: what's the dumbest alternative? Did I check it? State limits.
  6. Distill: write it up clearly, lead with the finding, show a few raw examples.

This is the shape of a strong application in miniature. Chapters 4–7 fill in each step's tools and craft.
