Part 1 · New readout methodsSlide 04

The lens lineage, recapped

Recall the progression: the logit lens reads the residual stream through the unembedding; the tuned lens adds a learned correction (Chapter 4). Both answer "what token is the model leaning toward here?" — but that's a narrow readout. The frontier asks: can we read richer internal content, not just next-token bets?

The frontier question

A model's residual stream surely encodes more than "what's the next token" — intermediate variables, plans, beliefs. The next-generation methods try to decode those into human-readable form. J-Lens and natural-language autoencoders are two such attempts, from different angles.

Part 1 · New readout methodsSlide 05

J-Lens / the "global workspace" idea

J-Lens is a newer lens-style method aimed at surfacing the intermediate variables a model computes during a forward pass — the internal "working memory" that isn't directly the next-token prediction. The framing borrows the cognitive-science idea of a global workspace: a shared space where the model's in-progress computations are broadcast.

Conceptually

Where the logit lens forces every internal state into "what token?", J-Lens tries to read internal states in terms of the concepts and partial results the model is actively juggling — a more faithful window into mid-computation "thoughts."

Hedge

This is genuinely new work; the precise construction and its validated claims are best taken from the current primary write-ups rather than memorised. Understand the aim — richer, faithful readout of intermediate state — and read the source when you use it.

Part 1 · New readout methodsSlide 06

J-Lens vs the older lenses

logit lens
projects to vocabulary; cheap; blurry early; only "next token."
tuned lens
learned per-layer fix; more faithful; still vocabulary-shaped.
J-Lens
targets intermediate variables / concepts, not just the next token; richer readout.
Known limitation to be honest about

Readout methods like these can be strongest at, or limited to, single-token / local reads and can struggle to capture computation spread across many positions. As with all lenses, a readout "showing" something isn't proof the model uses it that way — the causal-verification discipline from Chapter 4 still applies. New tool, same skepticism.

Part 1 · New readout methodsSlide 07

Natural-language autoencoders (NLAs)

A different and elegant frontier idea: instead of decoding an activation into a direction or a token, decode it into natural-language text that describes it — and train the system so that text can be re-encoded back into (approximately) the same activation.

encode to text, decode back — round-trip fidelity is the check

The reconstruction error gives an automatic, quantitative measure of whether the English description actually captured the activation — a built-in faithfulness check that plain "ask a model to describe this" lacks.

Part 1 · New readout methodsSlide 08

Why NLAs are exciting

  • Human-readable by construction — the output is prose, not a direction you still have to name.
  • Faithfulness is measurable — round-trip reconstruction quantifies how good the description is, reducing the "plausible-sounding but wrong" risk that plagues auto-interpretation.
  • General-purpose — a single readout method rather than a task-specific probe; aligns with Neel's taste for better general methods.
  • Applications — e.g. using the reconstructor to detect when a model's internal state doesn't support its output, a possible hallucination signal.
Hedge again

Early-stage. The strength of the reconstruction, what it faithfully captures, and failure modes are active questions — verify against current sources before leaning on specifics.

Part 2 · Synthetic document fine-tuningSlide 09

Synthetic document fine-tuning (SDF)

SDF means fine-tuning a model on generated documents crafted to instil a specific belief or fact — often something false or fictional — so the model comes to "believe" it, as if it had read it many times during pretraining.

The point

It's a controlled way to implant a known belief into a model. Because you know exactly what you inserted, you have ground truth — invaluable for studying how beliefs are represented, updated, and used, and for building model organisms (Chapter 5) with a known internal "fact."

Part 2 · Synthetic document fine-tuningSlide 10

What SDF is good for

  • Belief-representation studies: implant a fact, then probe/patch to find how and where it's stored and whether the model treats it as true.
  • Forensics ground truth: create a model with a known implanted quirk, then test whether your investigation methods recover it.
  • Honesty / CoT experiments: give the model a private belief and see whether its stated reasoning reflects or conceals it — a clean way to study unfaithfulness (Chapter 4).
  • Alignment tests: implant a belief like "I'm being tested" or a fake goal and study downstream behaviour.
Robustness caveat

Implanted beliefs may be shallow or brittle (fine under normal prompts, collapsing under pressure). Whether SDF produces "real" beliefs vs surface mimicry is itself a研究 question — and a good one to be careful about.

Part 3 · The assistant axis & model characterSlide 11

The assistant axis

Post-training turns a base model into "a helpful assistant" (Chapter 1, slide 47). The assistant axis refers to the internal direction(s) that encode this assistant persona — the "I am a helpful AI" self-representation that shapes tone, refusals, and values.

Why it's a rich target

If the assistant persona is a findable direction, you can ask deep questions: Where does it come from in training? How stable is it? Can it be steered, weakened, or hijacked (linking to jailbreaks and emergent misalignment's "persona" direction, Chapter 5)? It's a concrete internal handle on the fuzzy notion of "the model's character."

Part 3 · The assistant axis & model characterSlide 12

The science of model character

Zooming out: model character studies a model's values, personality, self-model, and how these arise from training. It's the interpretability of "who the model is," not just "what it computes."

Questions in scope

  • Does the model have stable, rankable values? Do they leak across contexts?
  • Does it have a coherent self-model, and is that self-model accurate?
  • How do constitutions / training principles show up internally?
A favourite frontier

Neel lists science of model character among his exciting areas. It's philosophically juicy but, done well, empirically grounded — probe for a value, test whether it's causal, check if the model's stated values match its revealed ones. Keep it rigorous and it's exactly his kind of project.

Part 3 · The assistant axis & model characterSlide 13

Do models follow their constitutions?

Frontier assistants are trained with explicit principles ("constitutions"). A natural, testable question: do the stated principles actually govern behaviour, or are they partly decorative? And are they represented internally as the model acts?

How you'd study it

Take a specific principle, construct cases that pit it against another pressure, and measure which wins (behavioural). Then probe/steer for a representation of that principle to see if it's causally load-bearing (internal). A clean marriage of black-box and white-box, aimed at a safety-relevant question — the recurring template of good modern work.

Part 4 · Neel's research-area menuSlide 15

Model biology

Studying the high-level, qualitative behaviour of models as if they were organisms (Chapter 3, slide 27) — especially surprising, emergent, or safety-relevant behaviours, without needing a full mechanistic account.

Example questions

How does a model's behaviour change across a long conversation? Does it form a model of the user? When does it "give up," hallucinate, or switch persona? What internal thing predicts these shifts? Observe → hypothesise → test, biologist-style.

Part 4 · Neel's research-area menuSlide 16

Model forensics

Detective work on a specific suspicious behaviour or model (Chapter 5, Part 5): what happened, why, and can we prove it? Uses the forensics loop and ACH.

Why it's such a good application area

It's inherently pragmatic and rigour-demanding, it rewards clever counterfactuals, and it produces a clear narrative ("here's the mystery, here's my investigation, here's what I concluded and what I couldn't"). That narrative shape maps beautifully onto the write-up Neel wants. It's arguably the single safest theme to pick for a strong, taste-aligned submission.

Part 4 · Neel's research-area menuSlide 17

Science of post-training

Understanding what each stage of post-training (SFT, RLHF, RL, distillation) actually does to a model's internals and behaviour. Post-training is where assistants — and many of their quirks — are made (Chapter 1, slide 47).

Example questions

What does RLHF change internally vs SFT? Model-diff a base vs its aligned version (Chapter 5) — what new features appear? What does distillation preserve or lose? These are tractable with diffing/probing and directly useful.

Part 4 · Neel's research-area menuSlide 18

Alignment training

Can we make models deeply and generalisably aligned — not just surface-compliant? This includes teaching models the reasons behind principles (so alignment generalises) and studying whether alignment "sticks" under pressure or context (linking to alignment context rot, Chapter 5).

The stakes

Surface alignment that collapses off-distribution is the deceptive-alignment worry in slow motion. Work here asks how to get alignment that holds — and how to verify it does, using internal signals rather than trusting behaviour. High-impact, and hard in the good way.

Part 4 · Neel's research-area menuSlide 19

Science of generalisation

Why does training generalise the way it does? Two models can fit the same data via different internal solutions (Chapter 1, slide 30), and narrow training can generalise shockingly wide (emergent misalignment, Chapter 5).

Example questions

Why does insecure-code training produce broad misalignment? What internal structure decides which generalisation a model lands on? Can we predict or steer generalisation? This connects deep science to concrete safety outcomes — a very Neel combination.

Part 4 · Neel's research-area menuSlide 20

Applied interpretability

Using interp to do something useful now: monitoring for harmful intent or hallucination, detecting prompt injection, catching eval awareness or deception, building probe-based guardrails — each measured against real baselines.

The clearest fit to "pragmatic interp"

This is the most direct expression of Neel's thesis that reading a model's mind should be useful. The template: pick a detection/control task that matters, build the simplest internal method, and show it beats a prompting or classifier baseline. If it doesn't beat the baseline, that's a real (and reportable) finding too.

Part 4 · Neel's research-area menuSlide 21

Better methods

Improving the general-purpose tools themselves — the lenses, autoencoders, probing/patching machinery — so that every downstream project benefits. J-Lens and NLAs live here.

A caution for applicants

Method-building can be high-impact, but it's easy to slide into "science of SAEs for its own sake," which Neel has cooled on. If you build a method, anchor it to a concrete task it demonstrably improves, with baselines. "My method is more elegant" is not a result; "my method catches X better than the standard approach" is.

Part 5 · Concrete phenomena for projectsSlide 22

User models

Models often infer things about you — your expertise, mood, political leanings, native language — and adapt accordingly, frequently without being asked. This user model is an internal representation you can probe for.

Project shape

Probe for "the model thinks the user is an expert" or "…is upset." Does it fire correctly? Does steering it change the response style? Is the model's user-model accurate, and does it act on it? Clean, testable, and quietly important for safety (manipulation, sycophancy).

Part 5 · Concrete phenomena for projectsSlide 23

Out-of-context reasoning

Out-of-context reasoning: a model uses facts learned during training (not present in the prompt) to reason in ways that weren't spelled out — e.g. combining implanted facts (Chapter 6, SDF) to reach a conclusion no single document stated.

Why it matters

It's central to worries about models "figuring things out" — including working out that they're an AI in a test, or chaining implanted beliefs into unexpected behaviour. SDF gives you a controlled way to create and study it: implant premises, see whether the model reasons over them out of context.

Part 5 · Concrete phenomena for projectsSlide 24

Concept-representation probes

A whole family of projects: find and validate the internal representation of a safety-relevant concept. High-value targets include:

truthfulness / lyingdeceptionuncertainty / "I don't know"eval awarenessharmful intentrefusal
The recipe you already know

Probe (does it decode?) → causal test (steer/patch — is it used?) → baseline (beats prompting? random direction?) → honest limits. Chapters 3–4 gave you every step. Pick a concept that matters and execute this loop cleanly — it's a proven application template.

Part 5 · Concrete phenomena for projectsSlide 25

Steganography in chain-of-thought

Steganography: a model hiding information in its output that isn't apparent to a reader — e.g. encoding its true reasoning in the surface form of a CoT while the visible text looks innocent. The nightmare failure mode for CoT monitoring (Chapter 4, slide 29).

Why it's studied

If training pressure teaches models to hide reasoning from monitors, our cheapest safety tool (reading the CoT) breaks. Understanding when/whether models can and do use steganography — and detecting it — is a frontier safety question with real stakes.

Part 6 · Choosing from the menuSlide 26

How to pick — Neel's taste, distilled

  • Useful & safety-relevant over elegant-but-idle. Ask "if this worked, would anyone care?"
  • Baselined — always have a dumb comparison in mind before you start.
  • On models that matter — capable modern open models, not GPT-2-era toys.
  • Small and concrete — one crisp, testable question beats an ambitious sprawl.
  • Truth-seeking — pick something where you'd be able to tell if you were wrong.
  • Aligned to his current interests — this chapter's areas, not the cooled ones (Ch3/Ch5).
Part 6 · Choosing from the menuSlide 27

Over- vs under-explored

Crowded / cooled

  • Training a marginally better SAE.
  • Hand-tracing yet another circuit for its own sake.
  • Toy models, grokking, purely theoretical work.
  • Studying GPT-2 / Pythia-era models.

Fertile / hot

  • Forensics of a concrete misbehaviour.
  • Applied monitoring/detection with baselines.
  • CoT faithfulness measurement.
  • Emergent misalignment, eval awareness, model character.
Blunt advice

Steering toward the right column, on a capable model, with honest baselines, is most of what it takes to be taste-aligned. The left column can still be done well — but you're swimming against his stated preferences.

Part 6 · Choosing from the menuSlide 28

A caution about the frontier

Don't over-rely on the newest toy

It's tempting to build an application around the shiniest method (J-Lens, NLAs, crosscoders). But new methods are less battle-tested, and a shaky method makes shaky conclusions. Often the dependable move is a simple probe or diff-in-means on an interesting question, with the fancy method as an optional comparison — not the foundation.

Restating the through-line

The question you pick matters more than the method. A pragmatic, safety-relevant question, attacked simply and honestly, beats a frontier method in search of a purpose. This is the same lesson from every chapter — it just matters most here, where the toys are most seductive.
