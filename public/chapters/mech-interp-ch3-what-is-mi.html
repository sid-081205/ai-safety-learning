Opening the black box

The mysterySlide 02

The mystery: nobody wrote the program

Imagine finding a machine that speaks fluent English, writes code, and passes exams — and when you open it up, all you find is billions of dials, each turned to some number. No labels. No manual. No author.

That's a trained model. We built the skeleton (Chapter 2's meetings and desks), but training turned the dials by itself. The billions of numbers work — we just don't know why.

the dials were set by training, not by any programmer
Mechanistic interpretability (this whole field) = detective work on the dials: finding the human-understandable ideas and mini-machines hidden inside — with enough evidence to be sure we're right.
The mysterySlide 03

Two ways to study the machine

Think of a doctor with a patient:

symptoms vs X-ray — both useful, different powers
A pro's attitude (Neel Nanda's, specifically)

Don't be a purist. Start with the cheap outside look — often it's enough. Reach for the X-ray machine when the outside view can't answer your question. The goal is understanding that's true and useful, not fanciness.

Clue 1 · Ideas are directionsSlide 04

What are we even looking for? Ideas.

When the detective opens the box, what's the thing to find? Not dials — ideas the model is tracking. Things like:

"this text is French""this is someone's name""the user is angry""this request is dangerous""we're inside a quote"

The field calls each of these a feature. Features are the nouns of interpretability — the units we hunt for.

The field's central bet

Underneath the billions of dials, the model is really built from features, plus wiring that connects them. If that's true, the model is understandable piece by piece. It's not guaranteed — but it's been true often enough to build a whole field on.

Clue 1 · Ideas are directionsSlide 05

Where ideas live: each idea is an arrow

Remember: inside the model, everything is a vector — an arrow in space. The big discovery is that each idea gets its own arrow direction, and "how much" of the idea is present = how far the model's current state points that way.

one arrow per idea; measure with a dot product (Chapter 1's similarity)

The classic party trick: king − man + woman ≈ queen. Arrows for ideas can literally be added and subtracted. This "ideas are arrows" claim has a formal name — the linear representation hypothesis — but the picture above is the whole content.

Clue 1 · Ideas are directionsSlide 06

Why the detective loves arrows

If ideas are arrows, you get two superpowers immediately:

reading = "probes" · pushing = "steering" — both arrive in Chapter 4
Keep one grain of salt

Not every idea is a neat straight arrow (some are curves or shapes — days of the week form a circle!). Treat "ideas are arrows" as a great first guess, not a law of nature. When it breaks, that's usually where something interesting is hiding.

Clue 2 · The overstuffed closetSlide 07

The plot thickens: too many ideas, too little room

The model wants to track millions of ideas — every language, every topic, every person, every vibe. But its space only has a few thousand dimensions… a few thousand "clean" slots for arrows.

Millions of ideas. Thousands of slots. Something has to give.

the storage problem every model faces
Clue 2 · The overstuffed closetSlide 08

The model's trick: squeeze the arrows together

Here's how the model cheats. Arrows don't need to be at perfect right angles to stay distinguishable — almost-right-angles works fine. And in high-dimensional space, you can pack in vastly more almost-perpendicular arrows than perpendicular ones.

this squeezing is called SUPERPOSITION — the single most important word in this chapter

Why does it work? Sparsity: at any moment only a handful of ideas are active. A sentence isn't about French and DNA and tax law at once — so the squeezed arrows rarely collide in practice.

Clue 2 · The overstuffed closetSlide 09

The price: everything looks tangled

The squeezing works great for the model — and terribly for the detective. Because arrows overlap, any single neuron you look at sits on top of several unrelated ideas and lights up for all of them.

Chapter 2's "messy filing" — now you know WHY: superposition causes it
The detective's rule that follows

Never trust "neuron N means X." It probably also means Y and Z. The meaning isn't in individual neurons — it's in directions you have to hunt for. (The hunting tools — probes and sparse autoencoders — come in Chapters 4 and 5.)

Clue 3 · The wiringSlide 10

Ideas connect into little machines: circuits

If features are the nouns, circuits are the sentences. A circuit is a chain: one part of the model writes an idea into the word's backpack, a later part reads it and acts.

a circuit = write an idea, carry it along the belt, read it later

You already know a real one! Chapter 2's copy-cat team (the induction heads): one head writes "the word before me was Harry," another head reads that note and copies "Potter" forward. That two-head chain is a circuit — found, understood, and verified inside a real model.

Why it mattersSlide 11

Why do all this detective work? Safety.

Here's the worry that funds the field: a very capable model could learn to act nice whenever it's being watched — and do something else when it isn't. Every test we run from the outside… it passes on purpose.

behaviour can be faked; internal machinery is much harder to fake

That's why "reading the model's mind" matters: things we'd love to catch from the inside include deception, hidden goals, "I'm being tested right now" awareness, and whether a nice-sounding explanation matches what the model actually computed.

Honesty checkpoint

Nobody has a reliable lie detector for models today. Interpretability is a promising tool, not a solved problem — and people who overclaim here are part of the problem.

Why it mattersSlide 12

Two mindsets: map everything vs. solve the case

the field started with the left mindset and has largely shifted right
Where Neel stands (matters for your project)

Firmly on the right. His line: "being able to read an AGI's mind should be extremely useful." Useful — measured against a simple baseline, on a model that matters — beats elegant. Frame anything you build as "here's a concrete question, here's my answer, here's proof it beats the obvious alternative."

The detective's standardsSlide 13

Watching vs. poking — the golden rule of evidence

Suppose you find a signal inside the model that lights up whenever it refuses a request. Nice! But do you know the signal causes the refusal? Not yet. Two levels of evidence:

the question that separates strong work from weak work: "did you poke?"
Our unfair advantage over neuroscience

Brain scientists can only watch. We can pause the model, edit any internal value, and re-run it — perfect experiments, any time. Chapter 4's main tools ("patching," "steering") are exactly this kind of poking.

The detective's standardsSlide 14

Beware the satisfying story

Detective's trap: the suspect who looks guilty, the story that fits perfectly… and is wrong.

Interpretability has famous cases of this — experiments that seemed to reveal a mechanism but were actually artifacts (your poke changed the output, yes, but through a totally different route than you thought). The field calls these interpretability illusions.

The one question that saves you

Before believing your own explanation, ask: "What's the dumbest alternative explanation for what I saw — and have I ruled it out?" Compare against something boring (a random direction, "just ask the model"). If your finding barely beats boring, say so.

Why humility wins

"Most research results are false, especially the exciting ones." A write-up that honestly states its limits reads as stronger, not weaker. Skepticism about your own work is the trait being selected for.

WrapSlide 17

The chapter in five sentences

  1. Trained models are machines nobody programmed — billions of dials set by training.
  2. Inside, the model tracks ideas ("features"), each stored as an arrow in space.
  3. It stores more ideas than it has room for by squeezing arrows together (superposition) — which is why single neurons look like nonsense.
  4. Ideas chain into little machines (circuits): write an idea, carry it, read it later.
  5. We do this because outputs can be faked but internals are harder to fake — and the evidence that counts is poking, not watching.
