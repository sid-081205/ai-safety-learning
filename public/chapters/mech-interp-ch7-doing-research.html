From knowing to doing

Chapters 1–6 built the what: the machine, the concepts, the toolkit, the phenomena, the frontier. This chapter is the how — the part that actually gets you selected.

The uncomfortable truth about the application

A capable AI model can write the code and run the techniques for you. So the application is not a coding test. It's a test of research judgment: can you pick a good question, design honest experiments, distrust your own results, catch your agent's mistakes, and communicate clearly? Everything here targets that.

Part 1 · The research processSlide 04

Explore — Understand — Distill

Neel's framework for a research project has three phases. Most people neglect the first and last.

They're not strictly sequential — you loop back — but knowing which phase you're in keeps you honest about what "progress" means right now.

Part 1 · The research processSlide 05

Explore: maximise information per hour

The goal of exploration is surface area — get your hands dirty fast and cheaply to discover what's interesting and what's tractable. Run the model, read outputs and chains-of-thought, poke it, plot things, follow surprises.

The mindset

Optimise for information gained per unit time. Prefer many quick, scrappy experiments over one elaborate pipeline. Chase anomalies — a weird output is a lead. Don't over-plan; the map is drawn by walking. This phase is where good taste in what to study develops.

Common failure

Skipping exploration and committing to the first idea. The best projects often pivot to something more interesting found while exploring. Budget real time here.

Part 1 · The research processSlide 06

Understand: hypotheses and honest tests

Once you have a lead, shift to understanding: form crisp hypotheses and design experiments that could disprove them. This is where the Chapter 4–5 toolkit and the forensics loop (with ACH) do their work.

  • State the hypothesis so specifically that an experiment could kill it.
  • Design the experiment that best distinguishes your top hypotheses (not just confirms your favourite).
  • Prefer causal tests (patch/steer) over correlational ones.
  • Keep a running list of alternative explanations and knock them down one by one.
The disposition being tested

Truth-seeking. "Most results are false, especially exciting ones." The researcher who tries hardest to break their own finding is the one Neel wants.

Part 1 · The research processSlide 07

Distill: the write-up is first-class

Distillation is turning tangled work into a clear, honest, communicable result — and it is not an afterthought. For this application, the write-up largely is the deliverable.

The most common own-goal

Brilliant work, rushed write-up. If the reader can't follow what you did and why it matters, the work might as well not exist. Distillation forces you to confront gaps — often you discover during writing that a claim isn't as solid as you thought. That discovery is the point.

Budget it

The application explicitly carves out separate write-up time on top of the research hours. Treat writing as real research work, not packaging.

Part 1 · The research processSlide 08

Research taste (and where a mentor adds value)

Research taste is the judgment for which questions are worth asking, which results are trustworthy, and when to pivot. It's the scarce skill — and the thing the application is really probing.

How to show taste under time pressure

Pick a question that is small enough to finish but interesting enough to matter. Show you considered alternatives and chose deliberately. Demonstrate you know what would make the result false. Taste isn't grand vision here — it's a hundred small good calls, made visible in your write-up.

Part 2 · Doing it wellSlide 09

Discipline 1: start simple

The escalation ladder from Chapter 4, restated as a research value: always try the dumbest thing that could work first.

Why it wins

Simple methods are faster, easier to trust, and set the baseline everything else must beat. Often the dumb thing just works and you're done. Reaching for SAEs when a prompt or a diff-in-means direction would do is a classic way to waste your limited hours and signal poor judgment.

Signal value

Neel explicitly rewards simplicity and "trying dumb things first." An application that shows a simple method working, clearly, beats one drowning in unnecessary machinery.

Part 2 · Doing it wellSlide 10

Discipline 2: baselines (the drum)

Top rejection reason

Failing to baseline is one of the most common reasons strong-looking applications get rejected. A result without a baseline is uninterpretable — you can't tell if it's impressive or trivial.

Every claim needs a "compared to what?": a random direction, a prompting baseline, a simpler method, chance performance, an ablation. Before you run the fancy thing, know the number the fancy thing has to beat.

The signature question

"Could I have gotten this by just prompting the model?" — the value-add over prompting Claude myself test. If the honest answer is "yes," report it. Honesty about a null result is worth more than a dressed-up non-result.

Part 2 · Doing it wellSlide 11

Discipline 3: truth-seeking & skepticism

The core scientific virtue for this field, stated bluntly by Neel: most research results are false, especially the exciting ones. Your job is to be the harshest skeptic of your own work.

  • When a result excites you, that's the cue to try hardest to break it.
  • Ask "what's the dumbest explanation for this?" and rule it out explicitly.
  • Watch for the ways you're fooling yourself: cherry-picking, p-hacking by trying many things, confirmation bias.
  • Prefer robust, boring-but-true over flashy-but-fragile.
How to show it

In the write-up, explicitly list what would falsify your claim, what you checked, and what you're still unsure about. Visible skepticism is a feature, not a weakness.

Part 2 · Doing it wellSlide 12

Discipline 4: sanity-check your agent

Neel's #1 piece of advice

You'll use AI agents to write code and run experiments. The single highest-value thing you can do is rigorously sanity-check what your agent produces. Agents confidently produce plausible, subtly-wrong results — and unchecked, those flow straight into false conclusions.

This is where the human adds value in an age of capable coding models. The agent is fast; you are the one who notices the metric was computed on the wrong split, the "effect" is a plotting bug, or the baseline was accidentally leaking.

Part 2 · Doing it wellSlide 13

How to sanity-check, concretely

  • Read the raw data. Actually look at individual examples, model outputs, and chains-of-thought — not just the aggregate metric. Most bugs are visible in the raw data.
  • Verify load-bearing numbers by hand. Recompute a key statistic two ways. If your headline is one number, be sure of that number.
  • Be suspicious of clean/too-good results. 100% accuracy usually means a leak or a bug, not a discovery.
  • Check the agent's code does what it says. Read it. Ask "what's the dumbest way this is wrong?" Test on a case with a known answer.
  • Re-run with a trivial control (shuffle labels, random direction) and confirm the effect vanishes.
The meta-point

Treat your own pipeline as a model organism you're doing forensics on. Same skepticism, turned inward.

Part 2 · Doing it wellSlide 14

Discipline 5: prioritise, don't rabbit-hole

With ~16–20 hours, time discipline is everything. The failure mode is polishing one sub-problem while the main question goes untested.

  • Regularly ask: "is this the highest-value thing I could be doing right now?"
  • Timebox exploration and infrastructure.
  • Prefer breadth-first: get a rough end-to-end result, then deepen.
Infrastructure trap

Don't spend 8 hours making a perfect experiment harness. Get something scrappy working, produce a result, and only invest in tooling that clearly pays for itself. Yak-shaving is the enemy of a finished project.

Part 2 · Doing it wellSlide 15

Discipline 6: know when to pivot

Not every lead pans out — that's normal and fine. The skill is distinguishing "this needs more digging" from "this is a dead end, move on," and not getting sunk-cost-anchored to a dying idea.

Heuristics

Pivot when: the effect keeps shrinking under scrutiny; every explanation you rule out reveals two more confounds; or you've spent your timebox with no traction. A clean pivot to a tractable question — clearly explained — reads as maturity, not failure. A forced, overclaimed result on a doomed question reads as the opposite.

Part 3 · Using LLMs wellSlide 16

LLMs as research collaborators

You're expected to use AI tools heavily — for coding, brainstorming, learning, and running experiments. Used well, they're a massive multiplier. The skill is being the senior researcher directing a fast, capable, occasionally-wrong junior.

What they're great at

Writing and debugging analysis code, implementing standard techniques, scaffolding experiments, explaining concepts, summarising papers, generating variations of a prompt or dataset. Delegate the mechanical; keep the judgment.

Part 3 · Using LLMs wellSlide 17

Fight sycophancy

Models tend to agree with you and validate your ideas — dangerous when you're trying to find the truth. An agent that says "great insight!" about a flawed hypothesis is actively harmful to your research.

Anti-sycophancy prompting

Ask for disagreement explicitly: "What's wrong with this idea?" "Argue the opposite." "What would a skeptical reviewer say?" "List the three most likely ways my experiment is buggy." Have the model generate competing hypotheses (ACH again). Treat its agreement as worthless and its critiques as gold.

Part 3 · Using LLMs wellSlide 18

Learn actively, not passively

Using an LLM to learn interp fast is great — but passively reading its explanations builds shallow understanding. Engage actively.

  • Ask it to quiz you; try to answer before revealing.
  • Explain a concept back to it and have it find the gaps.
  • Ask "why" until you hit the real reason, not a plausible-sounding one.
  • Verify its claims against primary sources — LLMs get interp details wrong.
Meta-note

This very course is an LLM-generated study aid. Use it as scaffolding, but pressure-test key claims against original papers and Neel's own writing — especially the frontier material in Chapter 6.

Part 3 · Using LLMs wellSlide 19

What LLMs are still bad at

  • Experiment design — they'll happily run a confounded experiment without flagging it.
  • Noticing the interesting anomaly — they optimise for your stated goal, missing the surprising side-observation that's the real find.
  • Knowing when a result is too good to be true — they rarely volunteer "this is probably a bug."
  • Genuine research taste — choosing what's worth doing.
This gap is your job — and your value-add

Precisely the things LLMs are bad at are the things the application scores. Your edge is judgment, skepticism, and noticing. Lean into them.

Part 3 · Using LLMs wellSlide 20

A fast iteration workflow

Practical setup that keeps you in a tight loop with the model and your agent:

  • Use a persistent notebook/kernel (Jupyter, or a tmux-persisted session) so activations and models stay loaded — reloading a big model every run kills momentum.
  • Let your coding agent operate against that live kernel so it can run cells, see outputs, and iterate — but you read the outputs too.
  • Keep a scratch log of what you tried and found; it becomes the skeleton of your write-up.
  • Use capable modern open models (Qwen, Gemma, DeepSeek families) — not GPT-2-era models, which are too weak to exhibit the interesting behaviour.

Part 4 · The applicationSlide 23

What good applications share (the rubric)

clarity
a reader with zero context understands what you did, found, and why it matters.
value-add
you did something beyond what prompting a model would give — human judgment is visible.
truth-seeking
you stress-tested your own claims and reported honestly, including negatives.
baselines
every result is measured against a sensible comparison.
simplicity
the approach is as simple as the question allows; no gratuitous machinery.
taste
the question is well-chosen — interesting, tractable, aligned to real interests.
technical soundness
the methods are correct and the code does what you claim.

Notice these are the same virtues drummed throughout the course — this slide is just their consolidation into a grading sheet.

Part 4 · The applicationSlide 24

Common mistakes that sink applications

  • No baselines — the number-one avoidable rejection.
  • Overclaiming — stating more than the evidence supports; ignoring alternative explanations.
  • Cherry-picking a few qualitative examples and presenting them as a general result (fine only if you're explicitly making an existence-proof claim — Ch3, slide 30).
  • Unnecessary complexity — reaching for SAEs/circuits when a probe or prompt would do.
  • Not sanity-checking the agent — shipping a plausible but buggy result.
  • Rushed, unclear write-up — great work the reader can't follow.
  • Wrong-taste topic — a beautiful project on something Neel has cooled on.
Part 4 · The applicationSlide 25

The executive summary (≤600 words)

The exec summary is what gets read first and hardest. Make it stand alone. A reliable structure:

  1. The question & why it matters (2–3 sentences). Safety relevance up front.
  2. What you did — the approach, in plain language.
  3. What you found — the headline result, with the key number and its baseline.
  4. A graph or two that carry the finding at a glance.
  5. Limitations & what you're unsure about — honest, specific.
  6. What you'd do next — shows research direction.
Graphs earn their space

Every figure should make one point clearly, with axes labelled and the baseline shown. A single clean, honest graph beats five confusing ones.

Part 4 · The applicationSlide 26

Writing for a reader with zero context

Assume the reader is smart, busy, and knows nothing about your specific project. Clarity is the single most-weighted virtue.

  • Lead with the finding, not the chronology. Don't make them read your whole journey to learn the result.
  • Define your terms and state the setup before the analysis.
  • One idea per paragraph; topic sentence first.
  • Show the load-bearing evidence (a table, a figure, a raw example), not just assertions.
  • Write the narrative: here's the question, here's what I did, here's what I found, here's why I believe it, here's what I don't know.
The test

Could a sharp person outside interp read your summary and correctly restate your finding and its caveats? If not, revise until they can.

Part 4 · The applicationSlide 27

Show your work — and negative results are fine

Two liberating truths

(1) A well-executed negative result ("I expected X, tested it properly, found no effect above baseline") is a genuine, respectable outcome — often more valuable than a shaky positive. (2) Showing your reasoning and dead ends demonstrates the judgment being graded. You're not selling a product; you're demonstrating how you think.

This takes the pressure off "getting a big result" and puts it where it belongs: on doing honest, clear, well-judged work. That reframing is both truer and calmer.

Part 5 · Putting it togetherSlide 28

A worked project shape

Concrete template you could adapt (an applied-interp / forensics flavour):

  1. Question: "Can a simple probe detect when [capable open model] is being sycophantic, better than just asking it?"
  2. Explore: generate sycophantic vs honest cases; read outputs; confirm the behaviour is real and elicitable.
  3. Understand: train a diff-in-means / probe direction; test it fires on held-out cases; steer it to test causality.
  4. Baselines: random direction; prompted-classifier ("is this sycophantic?"); input-length control.
  5. Skeptic pass: dumbest alternative? necessity + sufficiency? leakage? sanity-check the agent's metrics.
  6. Distill: ≤600-word summary, one clean graph (probe vs baselines), honest limits, next steps.

Small, finishable, safety-relevant, baselined, honest — the whole course in one project.

Part 5 · Putting it togetherSlide 29

A suggested 20-hour shape

~2–3 h
focused reading + picking a question (cap reading at 5h total across the project).
~4–5 h
explore: get the behaviour reproduced, scrappy first experiments, follow surprises.
~6–7 h
understand: main experiments, causal tests, and — critically — baselines.
~2–3 h
skeptic pass: sanity-check everything, rule out alternatives, re-run controls.
~2 h
distill: write the summary, make clean graphs, state limits.
Protect the last third

The temptation is to research until hour 20 and slap together a write-up. Don't. Reserve the skeptic pass and the distillation — they're where results become trustworthy and legible, and where most of the grade actually lives.

Part 5 · Putting it togetherSlide 30

Self-assessment checklist

Before you submit, can you answer yes to all of these?

  • Is my question safety-relevant and aligned with Neel's current interests?
  • Does every claim have a baseline it beats (or an honest "it doesn't")?
  • Did I test causally where I claim causation?
  • Did I sanity-check the agent's code and numbers, and read raw data?
  • Did I try to break my own result and report what I found?
  • Could a zero-context reader follow my summary and restate my finding + caveats?
  • Is my approach as simple as the question allows?
  • Did I state limitations and next steps honestly?
